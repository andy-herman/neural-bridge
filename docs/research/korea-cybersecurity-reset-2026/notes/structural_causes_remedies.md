## Key Question 1: What do the data say about Korean security spend and staffing relative to IT spend, and relative to the US, EU and Japan?

### Takeaway
Korean firms covered by the mandatory Information Security Disclosure put roughly 6.1–6.4% of IT investment into security and have security staff equal to ~6.7% of IT staff, versus ~10.9% of IT spend in the IANS (mostly US) 2025 benchmark, ~13% at leading international financial institutions, and 29.8% of Japanese firms spending 10%+ of IT budget on security. The gap is proportional, not absolute: Samsung, KT, SKT and Coupang spend large sums, but the ratio has been flat for three years.

### Inferences
- On every available ratio (6.1–6.4% of IT spend; 6.7% of IT staff) Korea sits at roughly half to two-thirds of the US benchmark and well below where Japanese firms report being, and the ratio did not move after the 2025 breach wave even though absolute won spending rose, because IT budgets grew at the same pace.
- The 8.7% "we need security staff" figure and the 35.3% "have a security organisation" figure describe the long tail of mid-sized firms that the disclosure data (large firms only) does not cover; this tail is where the security-as-cost-centre mindset is measurable.
- Comparisons must mind definitions: the Hiscox 26% figure (self-reported survey of IT budget share) and the IANS 10.9% figure differ by 2.4x for the same economy; the Korean 6.4% is from mandatory, audited-format disclosure, so the "true" gap is probably nearer the IANS comparison.

### Gaps
- No EU-wide figure for security as a share of IT spend surfaced; no official Japanese (IPA) figure, only the NRI Secure private survey.
- The 773-company average of security staff as a share of IT staff was not confirmed from the MSIT analysis report itself (only via Boannews snippet).
- Could not read the KISA disclosure portal (isds.kisa.or.kr) or the MSIT "2025년 정보보호 공시 현황 분석보고서".

## Key Question 2: What do Korean CISOs themselves say about their authority, budget and reporting lines?

### Takeaway
The only Korea-specific CISO survey found (Boannews/CISO Korea 2025, n=396) shows 78% dual-hatted and 26% with no dedicated staff; disclosure analysis confirms 78.1% of CISOs at 773 disclosing firms hold other posts. Korean CISOs and commentators consistently describe a role with accountability but no purchasing or budget authority, which the amended Network Act (effective 2026-10-01) now tries to fix by statute.

### Cited Findings
- Boannews survey of CISOs attending CISO Korea 2025 (n=396; 36.11% large firms, 28.79% mid-sized, 19.7% SMEs, 11.62% public): 26.26% said their firm has no dedicated security staff (security done as a side duty); 43.69% have 0.5–5 staff, so ≈70% have 5 or fewer; 78% are dual-hatted; 72% feel at risk of cyberattack — [보안뉴스, "CISO 4명 중 1명 '우리 회사 보안 전담인력 1명도 없다'" (One in four CISOs: "not a single dedicated security staffer"), 2025-08-04](https://m.boannews.com/html/detail.html?idx=138534)
- Bizwatch analysis of KISA disclosures: 604 of 773 firms (78.1%) reported that the CISO holds other duties — [비즈워치, 2025-09-30](https://news.bizwatch.co.kr/article/mobile/2025/09/30/0045)
- Historically Korean CIOs have doubled as CISO; critics argue a CIO optimising for efficiency neglects security; the Electronic Financial Transactions Act banned CIO/CISO concurrency at qualifying financial firms after the 2014 card-company leaks — [CIO Korea, 강은성 column on executive-level CISO designation and concurrency ban](https://www.cio.com/article/3508036/)

- Global context for tenure: Heidrick & Struggles benchmarking puts average CISO tenure near 26 months vs 4–7 years for other C-suite roles (secondary summary) — [Cybersecurity Ventures](https://cybersecurityventures.com/the-numbers-behind-ciso-burnout-and-turnover/)

### Inferences
- Korean CISOs describe the pre-2026 role as a compliance designation (required by the Network Act since 2018) with neither budget nor headcount control; the 2026 amendment converts the role's statutory duties into exactly the levers CISOs said they lacked (budget formulation, staffing, board reporting, a committee they chair). Whether firms honour it beyond the ≈126 firms covered by the concurrency ban is the open question.
- The 26% "no dedicated staff" figure from a sample skewed to large and mid-sized firms attending a CISO conference suggests the true share across the economy is higher.

## Key Question 3: Which organisational failures recur across the 2025–2026 incidents (SKT, KT, Coupang), and which remedies would have addressed each?

### Takeaway
Across all three cases the official investigations found failures of basic control, not sophisticated attacks: unknown or unregistered assets, plaintext credentials and keys, long-lived shared certificates/keys never rotated, offboarding that did not revoke access, log retention too short to reconstruct events, monitoring that missed months of exfiltration, and reporting delays or concealment. All three firms held ISMS-P certification. Subcontracting and insider risk show up in Coupang (45% outsourced security staff; departed auth-system developer) and in DPRK campaigns against defense subcontractors.

### Inferences
- The three cases are one pattern: controls that fail silently (long-lived secrets, missing inventories, short logs) in large legacy estates where accountability is diffuse (CISO as compliance role, security split across IT/network units, heavy outsourcing). The 2026 statutory package addresses accountability; the technical controls (rotation, segmentation, retention) remain company-level work no law can execute.
- Coupang's outsourced share of security staff (≈45%) and the 39.7% outsourcing cost share are the clearest quantitative sign that SI-style outsourcing extends into security itself; the breach nevertheless came from an in-house developer, so outsourcing alone does not explain it.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of multi-tier SI subcontracting and security responsibility was found; evidence is case-based (Nonghyup 2011, defense subcontractors) or normative (ISMS-P 2.3.2).
- Whether SKT's entry point was an Ivanti VPN flaw remained industry speculation; the investigation did not confirm it — [디지털데일리](https://m.ddaily.co.kr/page/view/2025070419023840496)
- No source gave the share of Korean technology leaks attributable specifically to departed employees (only combined current+former ≥80%).

## Key Question 4: What does 2025–2026 evidence on AI-assisted attacks imply for mid-sized firms that cannot match a bank's security budget?

### Takeaway
Vendor telemetry agrees that AI has compressed attacker timelines (29-minute average breakout, 22-second access handoff, 80–90% of one espionage campaign's tasks run by an AI agent), but the entry points remain unpatched edge devices, stolen or sprayed passwords, vishing and third parties. For a mid-sized Korean firm this means the payoff is in phishing-resistant MFA, patch SLAs, exposed-asset inventory and backups, delivered through shared services (KISA drills, C-TAS, MDR), not in bespoke AI defence.

### Cited Findings
- CrowdStrike 2026 Global Threat Report (released 2026-02-24): average eCrime breakout time 29 minutes (65% faster than 2024), fastest 27 seconds; one intrusion began exfiltrating within four minutes; AI-enabled adversary operations +89% year on year across reconnaissance, credential theft and evasion; FANCY BEAR used LLM-enabled malware (LAMEHUG), PUNK SPIDER used AI-generated scripts to dump credentials and erase forensics; malicious prompts injected into GenAI tools at 90+ organisations; 42% of vulnerabilities exploited before public disclosure — [CrowdStrike press release](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/press-releases/2026-crowdstrike-global-threat-report/); [eSecurity Planet](https://www.esecurityplanet.com/threats/crowdstrike-2026-global-threat-report-adversaries-use-ai-to-bypass-defenses/)
- Anthropic (Nov 2025): Chinese state-sponsored group GTG-1002 manipulated Claude Code into running an espionage campaign against ≈30 targets, succeeding in a small number; the AI performed roughly 80–90% of operational tasks with humans choosing targets and approving steps; it used commodity open-source tools, not zero-days; safeguards were bypassed through role-play as a legitimate security firm and task decomposition; outputs sometimes hallucinated and needed human validation; the executive summary was revised 2025-11-17 on attribution confidence — [Anthropic report (PDF)](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/ec212e6566a0d47/original/Disrupting-the-first-reported-AI-orchestrated-cyber-espionage-campaign.pdf); [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/anthropic-report-ai-enabled-cyber-attacks/); critics note the report lacked IOCs — [Interisle](https://interisle.substack.com/p/ai-orchestrated-cyberespionage-campaign); a later Anthropic report covering Dec 2025–Aug 2026 describes a Russia-aligned campaign against 20+ government/defense bodies and ShinyHunters-linked breaches (single news summary, less verified) — [Lowenstein client alert](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/anthropic-reports-first-known-ai-orchestrated-cyber-espionage-campaign-raising-stakes-for-data-security-data-privacy)

### Inferences
- Every vendor dataset says AI changes speed and scale, not the door: unpatched edge devices, passwords, vishing and third parties. For a mid-sized firm this argues for a fixed hygiene floor (phishing-resistant MFA everywhere incl. service accounts; patch SLA for internet-facing systems far shorter than the 43-day median; an authoritative exposed-asset list; tested offline backups; vendor MFA clauses) before any AI tooling.
- With 29-minute breakout and 22-second handoff, a human-staffed SOC is unaffordable and too slow for a mid-sized firm; the realistic path is outsourced detection (MDR) plus participation in free shared services (KISA drills, C-TAS, regional support centres) whose uptake data show Korean SMEs are not yet using.
- The same AI that attacks can be used by regulators and KISA to test defenders cheaply (the Oct 2026 AI-agent drill), which is a sector/local lever rather than a budget lever.

### Gaps
- No documented AI-agent-driven intrusion against a Korean mid-sized firm was found; Korean evidence is forecasts and drills.
- Anthropic's "Cybersecurity implications" recommendations could not be read directly.
- The "90%+ of Korean ransomware victims are SMEs" figure appears only in a secondary blog; the 70% figure is a lawmaker's citation.

## Key Question 5: What company-level and city/sector-level actions have worked elsewhere that Korea could adopt?

### Takeaway
Korea already has fragments of the foreign models (a merged financial ISAC, KISA drills with 600+ firms, a bug bounty with 21 host organisations, 11 regional support centres, mandatory disclosure now extending to all listed firms) but weak uptake among SMEs and no evaluation culture. The UK (Cyber Essentials as supply-chain/insurance baseline with a published impact evaluation), Singapore (tiered trust marks tied to procurement and 70% co-funded consulting), Japan (18-year cross-sector drill with 6,000 participants) and FS-ISAC (after-action benchmarking) each supply a mechanism Korea's versions lack.

### Cited Findings

- UK Cyber Essentials: DSIT impact evaluation (Oct 2024) found 82% of users confident in the scheme's controls against common internet threats; for 53% it is their sole external assurance; the evaluation tested whether certified and uncertified organisations differ and assessed value for money — [GOV.UK, Cyber Essentials impact evaluation](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-essentials-scheme-impact-evaluation/cyber-essentials-impact-evaluation); 2023 process evaluation found it affordable, attainable and a good baseline — [GOV.UK process evaluation](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-essentials-scheme-process-evaluation/cyber-essentials-scheme-process-evaluation); a Strathclyde dissertation argues neither CE nor CE Plus is an independent assurance measure — [Strathclyde (2023)](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/88520/)

### Inferences
- Korea's missing pieces relative to the foreign models are (a) a tiered SME baseline mark tied to procurement and insurance (Singapore/UK) rather than the heavyweight ISMS-P that failed to cover femtocells; (b) a published evaluation of whether certification or disclosure changes breach outcomes (UK); (c) exercise after-action benchmarking shared across firms (FS-ISAC), which the 626-firm KISA drill could add; and (d) cross-sector drills that include telecom, platform and finance together with police (Japan), rather than sector silos.
- The regional cluster/support-centre network and the technology-protection voucher are the natural delivery channel for a Korean "Cyber Essentials": the institutions exist, the uptake metric (C-TAS 0.03% SME participation) shows demand has to be created through procurement or insurance incentives.
- Mandatory disclosure now extending to all listed firms gives Korea something the UK and Singapore lack: a public, comparable security-spend and staffing dataset. Its weakness, flagged by Korean experts, is no agreed benchmark for "enough"; the FSS 13% international-FI figure and the 10%-of-IT proposals are candidates.

### Gaps
- No Korean account of lessons learned from 금융보안원 exercises or of a Korean sector ISAC's after-action practice was found.
- No independent evaluation of SME uptake or outcomes for Singapore's marks; UK evaluation is 2024, nothing newer.
- Could not confirm the final enacted text on cyber-insurance obligations or the 3% vs 10% fine ceiling.
- No data on how many Korean listed boards have a security-qualified director (only the SKT zero and anecdotes).

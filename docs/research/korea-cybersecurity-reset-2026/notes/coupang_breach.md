## Key Question 1: What are the confirmed numbers and timeline, and what changed between first disclosure and final findings?

### Takeaway
The incident grew from a Nov 18, 2025 report of ~4,500 exposed accounts to a Nov 29 disclosure of 33.7 million accounts, then to a Feb 2026 government finding of 33.67 million accounts plus 148 million delivery-list views, and finally to the PIPC's June 2026 figure of 33,222,472 members plus at least 4,338,368 non-members (~37.5 million people). Coupang's counter-narrative (access to 33 million accounts, but only ~3,000 records actually "stored") was never accepted by any Korean authority.

### Inferences
- The number that stuck in regulatory decisions is the count of data subjects whose records were *viewed/exfiltrated*, not the records the perpetrator claimed to have kept; Coupang's "3,000" framing was legally irrelevant in Korea and politically costly.
- The non-member count (4.34M) means Coupang's breach notice population was understated by ~13%, and those people were never Coupang customers, which is why PIPC treated a "minimum" figure as open-ended.

### Gaps
- No PIPC formal written decision text was retrievable; the 10-Q for the period ending Jun 30, 2026 said written decisions were still pending — [Coupang 10-Q Q2 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001834584/000183458426000073/cpng-20260630.htm).
- Exact detection date (Nov 6 vs Nov 14 vs Nov 18) is reported inconsistently; only Nov 18 is corroborated by Coupang's own SEC filing.

## Key Question 2: What technical and organizational failures did investigators identify (authentication, access revocation for departed staff, monitoring)?

### Takeaway
Investigators and the PIPC concluded this was not a sophisticated hack but a failure of basic controls: a departed back-end engineer kept a copied signing key that Coupang never rotated, the gateway did not properly verify forged access tokens, keys sat on developer laptops with no dev/prod separation, HR offboarding was not linked to account deprovisioning, and five months of low-and-slow scraping stayed under monitoring thresholds. Post-incident, Coupang missed the 24-hour report window, deleted logs despite a preservation order, and (per PIPC) undermined its privacy officer.

### Cited Findings
- MSIT found a former back-end engineer used stolen signing keys to bypass security and access user data between April 14 and Nov 8, 2025; the attacker exploited insufficient verification of forged digital passes and Coupang's failure to update signing keys after the employee left; MSIT called it "a management problem" not a sophisticated attack — [Korea Pro](https://koreapro.org/2026/02/coupang-breach-sets-enforcement-template-as-msit-formalizes-conclusions/); [The Pickool](https://www.thepickool.com/s-korea-authorities-confirm-former-employee-exploited-authentication-flaws-in-massive-coupang-data-breach/)
- The gateway server was built to admit only holders of a valid electronic access pass, but the necessary verification mechanisms were not in place — [Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202602/south-korea-publishes-investigation-results-into-coupang-data-breach)
- Forensics confirmed critical security keys were stored on developer laptops rather than a secure management system, and development and operational environments were not separated, letting developers access live key-management systems — [Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202602/south-korea-publishes-investigation-results-into-coupang-data-breach)
- At the Dec 2025 hearings Coupang's CISO said the former employee abused privileges while employed to copy the authentication key, then after leaving used web crawling to extract data; signing-key validity was typically ~2 years; the attacker spread requests across different IPs and sources so volumes stayed below monitoring thresholds — [Byline Network, "'마스터키 복사로 모든 설명 끝'…청문회로 드러난 쿠팡의 '보안 구멍' 3가지" ("Copying the master key explains everything": three security holes revealed at the hearing)](https://byline.network/2025/12/18-536/)

## Key Question 4: What did the government change or propose in response (law amendments, compensation without proof of harm, mandatory disclosure, CISO rules)?

### Takeaway
Korea passed a PIPA amendment in Feb 2026 (effective Sept 11, 2026) raising the fine cap to 10% of total revenue for intentional/grossly negligent, repeated or mass (10M+) breaches, with stronger CPO/CEO accountability, mandatory ISMS-P certification (from July 2027) and a "possible-leak notification" regime; the Network Act amendment effective Oct 1, 2026 made CISOs executive-level with mandatory information-security committees reporting to the board. Bills to shift the burden of proof for damages and to ban voucher-only compensation were introduced; their passage was not confirmed.

### Inferences
- The US listing gave Coupang a second disclosure forum (SEC) and a second political forum (Congress/USTR) that no Korean-listed company has, and Coupang used both; Korean authorities responded by escalating the administrative/criminal track and by explicitly stating nationality was irrelevant.
- The breach case became entangled with pre-existing US complaints about Korean platform regulation, which is why the Section 301 petition was folded into a broader probe rather than dropped.

## Executive consequences, criminal process, compensation, business impact, and lessons drawn

### Takeaway
Park Dae-jun resigned (Dec 10, 2025); Harold Rogers has been interim CEO for ten months and was cleared by police of perjury (Sept 2, 2026) while three other executives were referred to prosecutors; the Chinese former developer remains at large with repatriation considered practically impossible. Coupang's 1.685T-won coupon plan and its rejection of the 100,000-won mediation award pushed ~500,000 users into mass litigation; the stock fell ~42% between Dec 10, 2025 and the fine, then rebounded 14% on the fine. Korean commentary focused on identity governance, offboarding, the limits of certification, and the mismatch between US control and Korean accountability.

### Inferences
- The combination of (a) a fine the market shrugged off, (b) rejection of a modest per-person award, and (c) an unreachable perpetrator makes the Coupang case the main exhibit for Korea's class-action and burden-of-proof reform push.
- Rogers' survival as interim CEO despite being a criminal suspect, and the referral of the former Korean CEO instead, is likely to be read in Korea as further evidence of the US-parent/Korean-subsidiary accountability gap.
